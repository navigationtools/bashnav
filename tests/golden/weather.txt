=== weather learn fluid ===

  AIR IS A FLUID, AND MOIST AIR IS LIGHTER
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Air is a fluid. At the speeds anything on a boat moves it barely
  compresses, so it behaves very much like water - and the weather is
  fluid dynamics happening to a very large, very thin bath.

  And here is the counterintuitive one:

    Warm MOIST air is LIGHTER than warm DRY air.

  Humid air feels heavy. It is not. At the same temperature and pressure
  a given volume holds a fixed NUMBER of molecules - so water vapour does
  not add molecules to the box, it replaces them:

         H2O  18      pushing out      N2  28
                                       O2  32

  Every water molecule that goes in evicts something heavier. Same count,
  less mass, lower density.

  This is the engine of everything else. Warm moist air is buoyant
  twice over - once for being warm, once for being wet - which is why the
  tropics convect so violently, and why a thunderstorm is a heat engine
  rather than a wind event.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: It is 30 C and very humid. Is that air heavier or lighter than 30 C and dry?

Lighter. Water molecules at 18 replace nitrogen at 28 and oxygen at 32, and a given volume holds a fixed number of molecules. Warm and wet is buoyant twice over, which is what builds thunderstorms.
=== weather learn data ===

  THE THREE KINDS OF WEATHER DATA
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Most sailors never learn this, and it decides how much to believe.

  OBSERVATION   an instrument reading of what is happening now.
                 Not a forecast - but it is true.

  ANALYSIS      a chart drawn from observations and then edited and
                 verified by a human forecaster. A surface analysis,
                 most national weather service products. The best
                 available picture of NOW.

  MODEL OUTPUT  a computer's projection. What sailors call a GRIB.
                 Nobody has looked at it.

  The trap is the third. A GRIB arrives in colour, at high resolution, out
  to seven days, looking authoritative - and it is a machine's
  extrapolation with no human in the loop. Sailors treat it as gospel.
  The rule: check you are looking at an analysis, not a model run.

  And it places this tool honestly. It works entirely in the first
  column. Every number in it is something you measured. It cannot
  forecast - but what it reasons FROM is the one category that is never
  wrong, and the only one still there when the antenna comes down.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: A seven-day GRIB and a 24-hour surface analysis disagree. Which do you believe?

The analysis. A human forecaster has looked at it and corrected it. The GRIB is a machine's extrapolation that nobody has seen, and the further out it runs the less it is worth.
=== weather learn tide ===

  THE ATMOSPHERIC TIDE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The sun heats the air, the earth turns under it, and a pressure wave
  travels westward with the sun. It is on your barometer, every day.

  The odd part: the dominant component is SEMIdiurnal - twice a day,
  not once. Why once-a-day heating produces a twice-a-day pressure signal
  is a genuine puzzle in meteorology.

    size in the tropics          about 1.4 hPa each way
    maxima                       about 1000 and 2200 local
    minima                       about 0400 and 1600 local
    falling off with latitude    1.4 hPa at the equator, 0.5 at 45 deg

  The tropics' size is Bowditch's: about 3 hPa from top to bottom, so
  1.4 each way. The fall-off with latitude is the rule this app uses.

  Which is a trap worth knowing about. In the tropics the glass
  falls two to three millibars between mid-morning and mid-afternoon
  every single day, in perfect weather. A sailor who does not know
  that reads a routine afternoon fall as a system approaching.

  This app corrects for it before saying anything about tendency, and
  tells you how much of the change was the tide. Give it your longitude
  - it needs local solar time to know where in the cycle you are.

  In the browser, the barograph on What the log says draws this wave
  behind your own readings. A trace that follows the wave is only the
  tide; one that leaves it is weather.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: You are at 12N. The glass has fallen 2 hPa between 1000 and 1600. Worry?

Probably not - that is very close to the daily atmospheric tide, which is about 1.4 hPa of semidiurnal swing in the tropics with a minimum near 1600. Correct for it before reading anything into it. At 50N the same fall would mean much more.
=== weather learn coriolis ===

  CORIOLIS, WHICH IS NOT A FORCE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The earth turns underneath you. Describe anything's motion relative to
  the ground and it appears to curve. The "force" is the bookkeeping
  entry for having chosen a rotating frame - nothing is pushing.

  Its size goes as sin(latitude):

    at the poles     full strength
    at 45 degrees    about 0.7 of it
    at the equator   ZERO

  Where the sine comes from, because it is not arbitrary:

  The earth's spin points along its axis. What can deflect something
  moving HORIZONTALLY is only the part of that axis pointing straight
  UP where you are standing - and that part is sin(latitude) of it.

    at the pole      the axis IS straight up, so all of it counts
    at the equator   the axis lies flat along your horizon, so none

  Nothing about the shape of the earth comes into this. It would be
  exactly the same on a perfect sphere.

  The same answer from the other side, which is the one that makes it
  feel inevitable: what matters to a moving parcel is how far it is
  from the AXIS, and that goes as cos(latitude). At the equator cos is
  at its maximum, so it is flat there - move a few degrees north or
  south and your distance from the axis barely changes, so your spin
  barely changes, so there is nothing to turn you.

  And read that ZERO honestly. At 5 degrees the factor is sin 5, which
  is 0.09 - under a tenth of polar strength. Small, not absent. The
  five degree rule below is a threshold for getting a storm started,
  not a line where the physics switches off.

  Two consequences: where a cyclone cannot form, and which way
  to turn for the low.

  First: no tropical cyclone forms within about 5 degrees of the
  equator, because there is too little there to spin it up. All that
  heat and moisture, and not enough rotation to organise it.

  Second: it is half of Buys Ballot, which is the most useful rule this
  app has. Stand with your back to the wind and the low is on your left
  in the northern hemisphere - and on your right in the southern, because
  the sense of the rotation reverses.

  Backed and veered, again. The surface wind always turns toward
  the low. In the north the low is on the wind's left, so the turn is
  anticlockwise - backed. In the south it is on the right, so the turn is
  clockwise - veered. More in the lesson veer.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Why does no hurricane ever form on the equator?

Coriolis goes as sin(latitude) and is zero there. All the heat and moisture in the world, and nothing to start it rotating. About 5 degrees is the practical minimum.
=== weather learn coriolis, deeper ===

  FROM OUTSIDE, IT IS ANGULAR MOMENTUM
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Stand off the planet and watch. In that frame nothing curves and
  nothing is pushing. What is conserved is angular momentum
  about the earth's axis.

  A point at latitude L sits a cos L from the axis, so the ground
  there is already running east at:

    equator        1,674 km/h
    30 degrees     1,450
    45 degrees     1,184
    60 degrees       837
    pole               0

  A shell in flight feels almost no torque about that axis - drag
  aside, gravity acts straight at the centre - so its own
  u x r is fixed. Carry it closer to the axis and it must run
  faster east; carry it further out and it must run slower.


  TWO SHELLS, FIRED NORTH AND FIRED SOUTH
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
    fired    distance from axis   ground below      the shell
    north    shrinks              east SLOWER       outruns it
    south    grows                east FASTER       lags it

  
     FIRED NORTH
         aim              GUN
          +-----* EAST     .
          |    .           .
          |   .            .
          |  .             .
          |  .            .|
          | .             .|
          | .             .|
          |.             . |
          |.            .  |
          .             .  |
          .            .   |
          .           .    |
          .    WEST  *-----+
         GUN              aim
                      FIRED SOUTH
  both are to the RIGHT of
  the line of fire
  drawn from 45 N

  The curve is a parabola because the sideways acceleration is steady
  along the track, so the offset goes as the SQUARE of the time of
  flight. Its SIZE here is scaled to the width of the picture; the real
  figure is two paragraphs down, and it is small.

  Fired north it lands EAST of the aim. Fired south it lands WEST.
  Going north, east is to your right. Going south, west is to your
  right. Both deflect right - and in the southern hemisphere
  both deflect left. That is Coriolis, with no force anywhere in it.

  The size is real and small, and it takes BOTH halves of the story.
  A 16 inch shell at full range - 38 km, about 90 seconds of flight -
  ends up 27 km nearer the axis. The ground there runs 7 km/h slower
  east; the shell itself runs 7 km/h faster to keep u x r. So it
  arrives 14 km/h ahead of the ground, not 7, and since that excess
  grows from nothing the offset is the mean of it: about 175 metres.

  Checked the other way, which is the form a textbook gives: the
  sideways acceleration is 2 x Omega x v x sin(latitude), here 0.044
  m/s2, and half of that times ninety seconds squared is the same
  175 metres. Counting only the ground slowing, and forgetting that
  the shell speeds up, halves the answer - which is the easiest
  mistake to make here, because the first half is the memorable one.


  FIRED EAST OR WEST, THE MOMENTUM STORY RUNS OUT
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Everything above is about changing your DISTANCE FROM THE AXIS, so
  it explains a shot fired north or south and says nothing at all
  about one fired east or west - which does not change that distance.
  Yet an east shot gets the same 175 metre sideways push. That looks
  like a contradiction and it is not: it is a second mechanism that
  happens to give the same answer.

  Fire east and you are now going round the axis FASTER than the
  ground is. A faster circle needs more centripetal force than
  gravity was already supplying, and what it does not get, it loses
  as an outward push. The push is away from the EARTH'S AXIS - not
  away from the centre, and not straight up.

  And that is the whole answer, because at latitude L the direction
  away from the axis is tilted. It points:

    up                   by cos L
    toward the equator   by sin L

  One push, resolved two ways. The equatorward part is an ordinary
  sideways deflection - south, which fired east is to your right -
  and it is 2 x Omega x u x sin L, the same size as before. The
  upward part reduces the shell's apparent weight, and that is the
  Eotvos effect.

  Checkable, and it checks exactly: for purely eastward motion the
  Coriolis acceleration is 2 x Omega x u at EVERY latitude, and its
  direction is the axis-outward unit vector (0, -sin L, cos L) in
  east-north-up to twelve decimal places. The two famous components
  are not two effects. They are one vector's two shadows.

  The symmetry is worth holding on to. The sideways part goes as
  sin L and the vertical part as cos L, so they trade places:

    on the equator   away from the axis is STRAIGHT UP
                     no sideways deflection, Eotvos at its largest
    at the pole      away from the axis is HORIZONTAL
                     all sideways deflection, no Eotvos at all

  A shell does not stay up to enjoy its lost weight, so the thing you
  measure is not height but RANGE. Lighter, it hangs on past the
  moment it should have landed: at 45 N our 16 inch shell carries
  169 metres further fired east and 169 metres short fired west.
  Due north or south, nothing - there is no east component to act on.


  AND THE SHELL'S OWN CLIMB AND FALL
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  One more, and it is the one nobody mentions. The same cos L term
  acts on the shell's VERTICAL speed, east-west:

    climbing   pushed west
    falling    pushed east

  Those do not cancel. The shell spends the whole flight above the
  ground, so the westward push acts on a rising shell for the first
  half and the eastward push on a falling one for the second, and
  what survives is the time the shell spent up there at all. For a
  90 second flight it is about 61 metres to the west, whichever way
  the gun is trained.

  It is the same physics as the eastward deflection of a dropped
  stone, with the climb run backwards. And note what it does NOT do:
  cos L is positive on both sides of the equator, so unlike the other
  two this term does not reverse with hemisphere. The southern
  hemisphere is therefore not quite the mirror of the northern.


  THE THREE, AND WHAT THE GUN CAPTAIN CORRECTS
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  A 16 inch shell, 38 km, 90 seconds, from 45 N:

    turning with the earth    176 m right    any bearing
    the Eotvos effect         169 m range    east-west only
    its own climb and fall     61 m west     any bearing

  The 175 above and the 176 here are the same quantity; the first
  came from speeds rounded to whole km/h.

  Which add up, by bearing, to two corrections:

    fired      across the track      along it
    north      115 m right           -
    east       176 m right           107 m long
    south      238 m right           -
    west       176 m right           107 m short

  So the deflection is NOT the same at every bearing after all - the
  turning term is, and the climb-and-fall term adds to it going south
  and subtracts going north. Both still deflect right, which is the
  lesson; they do not deflect right by the same amount.

  All three are first-order, in vacuum, over a flat earth - the same
  idealisation as the 38 km in 90 seconds. Air resistance makes the
  climb and the fall unequal and moves the third figure in
  particular. Real firing tables are built from measurement.

  ONE THING THIS DOES NOT TOUCH: the wind. Air moving horizontally
  has no vertical speed worth the name and no east-west component
  going round the axis fast enough to matter, so of the three only
  the turning term applies to it. Everything below stands.

  Not to be confused with DRIFT, which in gunnery means the sideways
  deviation from the shell's own spin on a rifled barrel. Long range
  tables correct for both. They are different things with different
  causes, and only one of them reverses with hemisphere.


  AND NOW THE WIND
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Pressure pushes air from the high toward the low. The instant it
  moves, the same bookkeeping turns it right, so it never runs
  straight down the gradient. It ends up going around:

    northern hemisphere   anticlockwise around a low
                          clockwise around a high
    southern hemisphere   both the other way

  Friction is what turns circling into weather. At the surface the
  air is slowed, feels less deflection, and pressure wins a little,
  so the flow spirals IN to a low and OUT of a high:

    in   ->  convergence  ->  air must rise  ->  cloud, and rain
    out  ->  divergence   ->  air must sink  ->  clearing, and dry

  That is why a low is wet and a high is dry. It is the same friction
  that puts twist in a sail - see gradient for the numbers.


  HOW FAR ACROSS, AND WHAT DOES NOT CHANGE IT
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  With a drag that goes as the wind, the balance solves exactly:

    tan(alpha) = k / f       speed / gradient = cos(alpha)

  and the pressure gradient cancels out of the angle. A deeper low
  gives you more wind at the same angle, not a straighter wind.

  That is worth dwelling on, because the opposite is the intuitive
  answer: surely a hard blow is dragged round into the isobars? No -
  and with the quadratic drag water really has, the angle GROWS
  with the wind. The strongest lows on earth have the largest inflow
  angles: a hurricane's boundary layer runs 20 to 30 degrees of it.

  Nor can centrifugal force be the reason, because in a frictionless
  balance the wind already follows the isobars exactly, at every
  gradient and every latitude. There is nothing for it to improve.

  What does set the angle is the surface you are over, and latitude.
  Take 15 degrees over water at 45 N and carry the same drag north
  and south:

    latitude   over water   over land
    10            48            71
    20            29            55
    30            21            45
    45            15            35
    60            12            30
    75            11            27

  Near the equator the air pours almost straight down the gradient
  into the low. At high latitude it nearly follows the isobars. Same
  physics as the two shells: less sin(latitude), less turning.

                           LOW side
  
  
                        ^ surface
                      //
                   ///
               ////
           ////
       ////
   ..//............................
  
     -------------->  HIGH side
     gradient wind
  
     crosses the isobar at 15 deg
     toward the LOW


  AND THE CENTRIFUGAL TERM CHANGES THE SPEED
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Round a low it points outward, so it ADDS to Coriolis against the
  pressure gradient and the wind comes out below geostrophic. Round
  a high it subtracts, and the wind runs above it. One isobar
  spacing, 4 mb in 100 km at 45 N, which is 62 knots geostrophic:

    curvature    round a low   round a high
    2000 km         54 kn         76 kn
    1000 km         49 kn         impossible
     500 km         43 kn         impossible
     250 km         36 kn         impossible

  That word is the arithmetic, not a shrug. For a high the balance
  needs f squared R to be at least 4G, and past that there is no
  solution at all - no wind can hold that pressure field up. So a
  deep tight low is ordinary and a tight intense high does not exist,
  which is why every chart you have ever read looks the way it does.

  And the limit tightens as you go equatorward, because it goes as f
  squared. At 1000 km of curvature the steepest high that can exist
  is 6.1 mb per 100 km at 75 degrees, 3.3 at 45, and 0.2 at 10. The
  tropics get their weather from lows for a reason.

  Honest boundary: these are steady-state balances with a drag law
  fitted to an observed angle, not a boundary-layer model. The 10
  metre wind is lower again than cos(alpha) says, because most of
  that reduction is shear between the anemometer and the gradient
  level rather than the balance above.

=== weather learn gradient ===

  GRADIENT WIND, SURFACE WIND, AND FRICTION
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Well above the sea the wind balances the pressure gradient against
  Coriolis and runs along the isobars. That is the gradient wind, and
  it is what a weather chart draws.

  Down where you are, friction slows the air. Slower air feels less
  Coriolis. So pressure wins a little, and the surface wind blows
  across the isobars, toward the low:

    over water   backed 10-20 degrees, and about 2/3 of gradient speed
    over land    backed 30-40 degrees, and 1/3 to 2/3 of gradient speed
    southern hemisphere: veered, not backed

  How much the land slows it depends on the ground. Open fields and
  farmland drag least; forests, towns and cities, with their trees and
  buildings, drag most, and turn the wind further across the isobars.
  These are rules of thumb. The sources disagree on the land figure,
  from 1/3 (Royal Meteorological Society) to 2/3 (US Navy training).

  Backed and veered, again. The surface wind always turns toward
  the low. In the north the low is on the wind's left, so the turn is
  anticlockwise - backed. In the south it is on the right, so the turn is
  clockwise - veered. More in the lesson veer.

    OVER THE SEA                     OVER LAND
                        LOW side                 ^       LOW side
                                                :
                                               ::
                                             ::
                   ^                        :
                  /                        /
              ////                       //
          ////                         //
      ////                            //
   ../..........................    .............................
  
    ---------------------->          ---------------------->
    the wind aloft                   the wind aloft
  
    backed 10-20 deg                 backed 30-40 deg
    about 2/3 of the speed aloft     1/3 to 2/3 of the speed aloft
                                     1/3 towns, forests; 2/3 open

  How strong, from the chart. The gradient wind's speed comes from
  how close the isobars are: the closer they are, the harder the push
  toward the low and the stronger the wind. Nearer the equator the
  earth's turn is weaker, so the same spacing blows harder. The scale:

    THE WIND ALOFT, KNOTS, ISOBARS 4 hPa APART
  
     spacing, nm    30 N    40 N    50 N    60 N
              50      94      73      61      54
              75      63      49      41      36
             100      47      37      31      27
             150      31      24      20      18
             200      23      18      15      14
             300      16      12      10       9

  At 100 nm apart and 50 degrees north that is 31 knots aloft, so about
  20 on deck over the sea. Measure the gap on your own chart and the
  screen The wind from the isobars works it for you, down to the deck.

  This is the chart-to-deck translation - what the isobars mean for
  what you will actually get on the wind instrument.

  And the same boundary layer does something else you already know about.
  Friction slows the lowest air, so wind speed climbs with height, and
  the apparent wind aloft is stronger and further aft than at the boom.
  That is why a sail is twisted. One piece of physics, two apps.

  Why it turns: three forces
  Three things act on the wind down here. The push from high pressure
  toward low - what the isobars show - drives it. The earth turning
  beneath it bends it: to the right in the north (Coriolis). And the
  drag of the sea or the land slows it.

  Aloft, with nothing to drag on, the bend grows until it matches the
  push, and the wind runs straight along the isobars. Down here the drag
  slows it, a slower wind is bent less, and the push toward the low
  wins: the wind slants in across the isobars. Drawn head to tail, the
  three close into a triangle:

    SEA: little friction             LAND: more friction
           LOW side                         LOW side
              ^                                ^
              |\                               |\\
              |\                               |  \
              | \                              |   \  Coriolis
              | \  Coriolis                    |    \\
    pressure  |  \                   pressure  |      \
              |  \                             |       \
              |   \                            |      //
              |   \                            |    //
              |   _                            | ///
              o___                             o/
            friction                         friction
    turned 15 deg toward the low     turned 35 deg toward the low
    friction lies along the wind, Coriolis across it
    more friction: a longer friction side, a shorter Coriolis side

  The rougher the surface, the longer the drag side and the shorter the
  bend: the wind turns further in toward the low, and slows. Over the
  sea, a little. Over towns and forests, a lot.
  The triangle is for the lowest kilometre or so of air. On deck the
  wind is slower again, because it drops toward the surface - the same
  drop that twists a sail - so the rule says about 2/3 over the sea.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: The chart shows a 30 knot gradient wind. What do you expect on deck, offshore, in the northern hemisphere?

Around 20 knots - roughly two thirds - and backed 10 to 20 degrees from the isobar direction, because friction slows the surface air, which weakens Coriolis, so pressure gradient wins a little and the wind blows across the isobars toward the low.
=== weather learn veer ===

  BACKING AND VEERING, AND WHY THE SOUTH IS THE OTHER WAY ROUND
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Two words a sailor uses all the time, and they have two jobs.

  Over time, they say which way the wind has shifted. The wind
  veers when it shifts clockwise round the compass: west, then
  north-west, then north. It backs when it shifts anticlockwise:
  west, then south-west, then south.

    THE WIND SHIFTS, OVER TIME
  
      backs: anticlockwise              veers: clockwise
  
    S  <--  SW  <--  W   -->  NW  -->  N
  
    compass words: the same north and south of the equator
  

  These are compass words, so they mean the same on either side of the
  equator. What changes is what a shift is telling you.

  What a shift tells you, in the north. Depressions here mostly run
  west to east. When one passes on your north side, as most do in
  the Channel, the wind veers as its fronts go through: south-west
  ahead of it, west at the warm front, north-west behind the cold front.
  When the centre passes on your south side, the wind backs
  steadily instead: east, north-east, north.

    looking the way the low goes: on its right the wind veers,
    on its left it backs - north and south of the equator alike

  One rule covers both. Look the way the low is going. On its
  right the wind veers; on its left the wind backs. A depression
  going east has its left on the north side, so a boat there has the wind
  back. A storm going west - in the trade-wind belt, before it turns - has
  its left on the south side, and a boat there has the centre pass to its
  north while the wind backs.

  South of the equator it runs the other way. The wind goes round a
  low clockwise there, so the same picture plays backwards. A low passing
  on your poleward side - the south - makes the wind back: north, then
  north-west, then south-west as the cold front comes through.

    looking the way the low goes: on its right the wind veers,
    on its left it backs - north and south of the equator alike

  Against the wind aloft, the same two words describe the friction
  turn from the gradient lesson. Down where you are, the sea slows the
  wind and it turns in toward the low. In the north the low is on the
  wind's left, so turning toward it is turning anticlockwise: the surface
  wind is backed from the isobars. In the south the low is on the right,
  so the same turn is clockwise: veered.

                        LOW side
                  surface            the wind aloft
                        //^          ---------------------->

  Why the south is the other way round, without the mathematics. The
  earth turns under the moving air, and that bends anything moving: to
  its right in the north, to its left in the south. So air drawn into a
  low swings round it anticlockwise in the north and clockwise in the
  south, and the low ends up on the wind's left in one and on its right
  in the other. Friction always lets the wind slip toward the low - and
  that is backed in one hemisphere and veered in the other.

  And the rule that carries all of it to the cockpit is Buys Ballot:
  back to the wind, the low is on your left in the north, and on your
  right in the south.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Off the Isle of Wight the wind goes from south-west to west to north-west over six hours, and the glass starts to rise. Backed or veered - and where did the low go?

Veered: it shifted clockwise round the compass. In the northern hemisphere that is a depression whose centre has passed on your north side, and the shift to north-west with a rising glass says the cold front is through.
=== weather learn lapse ===

  LAPSE RATES, STABILITY, AND THE CLOUD BASE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Air cools as it rises, because it expands. How fast is the whole game.

    dry adiabatic     about 3.0 C per 1000 ft   an unsaturated parcel
    saturated         about 1.5 C per 1000 ft   condensing gives heat back
    environmental     whatever the air actually does - measured

  Compare the environmental rate with the adiabatic and you have
  stability. If the surrounding air cools FASTER with height than the
  parcel does, the parcel stays warmer than its surroundings, keeps
  rising, and you get towering cloud. That is instability, and it is why
  a saturated parcel can keep going where a dry one would have stopped.

  THE CLOUD BASE falls straight out of it, and the arithmetic is
  something you can do on deck:

    a rising parcel cools at         3.0 C / 1000 ft
    its dew point falls at           0.5 C / 1000 ft
    so the spread closes at          2.5 C / 1000 ft

    height (ft) = (air temp - dew point, in C) x 400

  400, not 333. It is not the lapse rate, it is the DIFFERENCE between
  two of them. Divide by the lapse rate alone and you put the base a
  fifth too low. And it is air temperature, not sea temperature - the
  sea is a fair proxy offshore and badly wrong near a front.

  A field of cumulus with flat bases all at one height IS that level made
  visible. Above it, the trade inversion caps the marine layer, and the
  flat tops are you looking at the underside of the lid.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Air 18 C, dew point 12 C. How high is the cloud base?

About 2,400 ft: a spread of 6 C, and the spread closes at 2.5 C per 1000 ft, so 6 x 400. Not 6 x 333 - the divisor is the DIFFERENCE between the parcel's lapse rate and the dew point's, not the lapse rate itself.
=== weather learn cells ===

  THE HEAT ENGINE, AND THE THREE CELLS
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The tropics take in more energy from the sun than they radiate away.
  The poles do the reverse. The atmosphere and the ocean exist, as far as
  weather is concerned, to move the difference poleward.

  Most of the atmosphere's share travels as latent heat - water
  evaporated in the tropics and released as rain somewhere else. The
  water cycle is not a side effect of the weather. It is the working
  fluid.

    Hadley    rises at the equator, descends near 30 degrees
    Ferrel    30 to 60, and the one you probably sail in
    Polar     60 to the pole

  The air descending at 30 degrees has already rained out everything it
  had, so it arrives warm and dry. That gives you the subtropical highs,
  the horse latitudes, the trade winds on their equatorward side - and
  most of the world's deserts sit at that latitude for this reason.

  And it closes the loop with the last lesson: that same descending
  warm air is the inversion capping the marine layer. The lid over your
  flat-topped cumulus is the far end of a circulation that started as
  thunderstorms on the equator.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Why are so many of the world's deserts near 30 degrees latitude?

That is where the Hadley cell descends. The air rose at the equator, rained out everything it had, and comes back down warm and dry. The subtropical highs, the horse latitudes and the deserts are all the same descending branch.
=== weather learn h500 ===

  THE 500 MILLIBAR CHART, AND THE 564 LINE
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  A 500 mb chart is a topographic map of a pressure surface: the
  contours are the HEIGHT at which the pressure is 500 millibars, drawn
  in decametres. So "564" means the 5,640 metre contour. Low heights are
  cold air, high heights are warm.

  It is around 18,000 feet, with about half the atmosphere's mass above
  and half below - which is why its flow is a fair proxy for where a
  whole system gets carried. The steering level.

  Five rules a mariner can use from one chart:

    1. The surface storm track lies 300 to 600 nm poleward of the
       5640 m contour, and runs parallel to it.

    2. All the gale force winds are poleward of the 564 line.
       Stay on its equatorward side and you stay out of the gales. This
       is the routing rule, and it is why the standard book on the
       subject is called Heavy Weather Avoidance.

    3. Surface lows and fronts move at a third to a half of the
       500 mb wind speed above them.

    4. Surface wind behind the system, in the cold air to the west
       and southwest, is about half the 500 mb wind speed.

    5. Tighter contours mean stronger wind - the upper-air version of
       close isobars. And the 588 contour marks the subtropical ridge,
       which is what steers tropical systems.

  You do not need the internet for this. A 500 mb chart comes in by HF
  radiofax, on an SSB receiver, at sea, with no connectivity at all. Read
  three numbers off it and weather chart will walk the rules with you.

  From the Mariner's Guide to the 500-Millibar Chart, by Joe Sienkiewicz
  of NOAA's Ocean Prediction Center and Lee Chesneau - US Navy, then OPC,
  who spent his retirement teaching this to sailors. See docs/SOURCES.md.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: The 564 line runs east-west 200 nm north of you. Where is the storm track, and are you in the gales?

The track is 300 to 600 nm poleward of the 564 line and parallel to it, so well north of you. And all the gale force winds are poleward of that line - so at 200 nm south of it you are on the right side of it. That is the routing rule.
=== weather learn cyclone ===

  HOW A TROPICAL CYCLONE IS MADE, AND HOW TO KEEP AWAY
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  This is the payoff for the other lessons: it needs all of them.

    warm deep water    about 26.5 C, and 50 m deep, or the storm
                       stirs up cold water and kills itself
    enough Coriolis    so not within ~5 degrees of the equator
    a moist unstable column  moist air is buoyant twice over
    weak wind shear    or the tower is torn apart before it organises
    something to start it  a wave, a trough, an old front

  Take any one away and you get a lot of rain and no cyclone. Which is
  useful: it tells you which conditions to worry about.

  AND AVOIDANCE IS A PROBLEM YOU ALREADY KNOW.

  A storm centre has a position, a course and a speed. So do you. You
  want the largest possible closest point of approach. That is exactly
  the relative-motion problem from colregs - the same vector triangle,
  the same bearing drift, the same answer. The dangerous semicircle is
  just the half where the storm's own motion adds to its wind.

  Which is why the tools in this suite keep turning out to be the same
  few ideas wearing different hats.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Sea surface 27 C, moist unstable air, a nice tropical wave - and 40 knots of shear aloft. Cyclone?

No. Shear tears the tower apart before it can organise. Four of the five ingredients is not a storm, which is exactly why knowing the list is useful.
=== weather learn seasons ===

  THE TILT, THE SEASONS, AND AN HONEST BOUNDARY
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  The earth's axis leans 23.5 degrees and keeps pointing the same way
  as it goes round. So each hemisphere leans toward the sun for half the
  year. That is the whole of the seasons - not distance from the sun,
  which barely varies and is at its smallest in northern winter.

  At sea you meet it as migration: the whole belt of weather moves north
  and south with the tilt. The equatorial convergence, the subtropical
  ridge, the storm tracks. A cyclone season is a season because the tilt
  makes it one - warm water and a ridge in the right place at once.

  Now the honest part.

  The axis also wobbles, a full circle every 26,000 years, and the orbit
  stretches and relaxes on cycles of 100,000. Those are real and they
  drive ice ages.

  They are climate, not weather, and nothing on your passage depends
  on them. They are in this lesson because they are interesting and
  because you asked, and they are labelled so you know which is which.

  Every other lesson here earns its place by changing what you would do.
  This half of this one does not, and pretending otherwise would be the
  beginning of a tool full of things that sound like knowledge.

  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Question: Northern winter is when the earth is CLOSEST to the sun. So why is it cold?

Because the seasons are the 23.5 degree tilt, not the distance. The northern hemisphere is leaning away, so the sun is low, the days are short, and the same energy is spread over more ground. The distance varies by about 3 percent and is swamped by it.
